I am an Associate Professor in the Department of Sociology at Boston College, where I study two questions: what people want from their work, and what work does to them in return. I take up these questions as work changes—where we do it, how long we spend on it, and what we expect from it—and trace how these changes shape people's attitudes, health and well-being, and lives outside work in the United States, China, and beyond.

I am currently working on three projects:

  • a study of remote and hybrid work during the COVID-19 pandemic (with Phyllis Moen; funded by NSF);
  • a project on the employee, organizational, and societal impacts of four-day workweeks (with Juliet Schor, Orla Kelly, and Phyllis Moen; funded by NSF and the Russell Sage Foundation); and
  • a study of how young people in China understand the meaning of work, drawing on in-depth interviews.

I received my Ph.D. in Sociology and M.S. in Statistics from the University of Minnesota.

Remote/Hybrid Work in Flux: Work-Place/Preference Mismatch and Adaptations
Social Forces · with Phyllis Moen
As employers first sent workers home and then called many back, we examine the gap between where people work and where they want to work—what we call work-place mismatch—and how workers respond: by changing where they work, changing what they want, or leaving their employer.

Work Time Reduction Via a Four-day Workweek Finds Improvements in Workers' Well-being
Nature Human Behaviour · with Juliet Schor, Orla Kelly, and Guolin Gu
Drawing on trials across organizations in multiple countries, we find that moving to a four-day week—with no loss of pay—improves workers' well-being, reducing burnout and lifting mental and physical health.
Journal

Stigma, Perceived Discrimination, and Mental Health during China's COVID-19 Outbreak: A Mixed-Methods Investigation
Journal of Health and Social Behavior · with Yue Qian and Yongai Jin
Drawing on a national survey with an embedded experiment and 50 interviews, we show how people tied to Hubei and those labeled as patients were stigmatized during China's outbreak, and how the discrimination they faced led to psychological distress.
Journal

Understanding Trends in the Concentration of Infant Mortality among Disadvantaged White and Black Mothers
Demography · with Liying Luo
We develop an index of how concentrated infant deaths are among unmarried and less-educated mothers, and show that it has stayed higher for Black than for White mothers over three decades, even as its trends differed between the two groups.
