Remote & hybrid work

Where do people want to work—and who gets to choose?

With Phyllis Moen · Funded by the National Science Foundation · National panel survey, October 2020–April 2022

The pandemic sent millions of office workers home almost overnight. Then employers began calling them back. Where work happens turned into a running negotiation between what workers want and what their jobs allow. We followed the same workers for 18 months to see how their preferences and their actual work locations moved apart, who could close the gap, and what the gap did to their working lives and well-being.

1 in 2

As many as half of the workers we followed were, at some point during those 18 months, not working where they wanted to work.

The gap is unequal

Hispanic workers, Black men, and workers without a college degree who prefer remote work are the most likely to be stuck on-site—and the least able to change where they work or to quit.

Going back raised the pressure

Compared with staying remote or moving to hybrid, returning to the workplace raised psychological job demands, and for women it cut decision latitude and schedule control—most of all for Black and Hispanic women.

Hybrid helped some more than others

Moving to hybrid work was especially good for the well-being of minority men and less-educated men, but not for white women.
