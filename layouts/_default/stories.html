Remote & hybrid work

What changes when work leaves the office—and for whom?

Data
Remote Work Dynamics Panel Study, October 2020–April 2022 (with Phyllis Moen)
Funding
National Science Foundation (2032639, 2049125)

The pandemic sent millions of workers home almost overnight. Then employers began calling them back. We followed the same workers for 18 months, through both moves, to see what changed—where they worked and wanted to work, their hours, their job conditions, their family lives, and their well-being—and how those changes differed by gender, race, education, and caregiving.

Where

As work moved home and then back again, many people found themselves working in a place they had not chosen. A mismatch like this is common and often temporary: people change jobs, change where they work, or change their minds. The trouble starts when they cannot do any of those things. Workers with the least bargaining power were the most likely to get stuck, and being stuck, more than the mismatch itself, is what took a toll on how they felt about their work and their lives.

Social Forces 2025 · Social Psychology Quarterly 2026 · Society and Mental Health forthcoming

Hours

Working from home is often pictured as a way to gain control over one's time. When we surveyed remote workers in October 2020, about half said their hours were about the same as before the pandemic. Averages, though, hide what happened to women: their hours were more likely to swing, sharply down for some and sharply up for others. Which way they went depended on care. Women with the heaviest care loads, such as those looking after both children and aging parents, put their jobs on the back burner. Women with lighter care duties often worked longer, possibly to make up for interruptions at home or to show they were still committed.

Work and Occupations 2022

The job

Offices have long run on face time and on an image of the ideal worker who is always present. Compared with going back, staying remote or hybrid eased job pressure and gave many workers more say over their work. Returning meant returning to the old rules, and the costs were not shared equally: Black and Hispanic women lost the most control over their schedules. The office did bring more support from coworkers, but mainly for white workers; Black men who returned got less.

Work and Occupations 2025

Home

Working from home has long been seen as a way to fit work and family together. The pandemic tested that idea under hard conditions. When schools stayed open, working from home eased mothers' stress; when schools were ordered to close, it added to it. Parents who worked remotely in jobs that were rarely done from home before COVID-19 had the hardest time. How people drew the line between work and home mattered too: those who set up physical boundaries fared best, while caregivers were the most likely to say they could not keep the two apart.

Social Sciences 2024 · ILR Review 2026 · Community, Work & Family forthcoming

Well-being

Remote work was often called a silver lining of the pandemic. Our findings partly bear that out: a full return to the office was generally more stressful than staying remote. Hybrid work, the middle ground, worked out differently for different people. It was especially good for minority men and men without a college degree, but worse for white women.

Journal of Health and Social Behavior 2023

Four-day workweeks

What happens when the workweek gets shorter but the paycheck doesn't?

Data
Four-day workweek trials, 2022–ongoing: more than 10,000 employees in more than 270 organizations in North America, Europe, Australia and New Zealand, and South Africa (with Juliet Schor, Phyllis Moen, and Orla Kelly)
Funding
National Science Foundation (2241840); Russell Sage Foundation (2205-38631)

Since 2022, hundreds of organizations have moved to a four-day, 32-hour week for six months while keeping pay the same. We survey their employees before, during, and after each trial, and we keep in touch with the organizations afterward. We ask what shorter hours change for workers—their health, how they spend their time, where they draw the line between work and the rest of life, and what they believe their workplaces expect of them—and what it takes for organizations to make the change last.

Well-being

Long hours are tied to burnout and poor health, yet workplace responses to overwork have often asked individual workers to cope better on their own. The four-day week changes the schedule for everyone at once. Workers in the trials reported less burnout and better job satisfaction, mental health, and physical health; workers in comparison companies that kept their usual schedule saw no such change. The bigger the cut in hours, the bigger the gains, and the gains held a year later. Why did fewer hours help? Three changes did most of the work: workers felt better able to do their jobs, slept better, and were less tired.

Nature Human Behaviour 2025

Keeping it

A trial is easy to start. The harder question is whether employers keep the new schedule after the trial ends, and most did. The companies that went back to five days tell us what makes the change last. Companies where work stress rose rather than fell were more likely to stop. So were companies whose workers reported working more efficiently by the end of the trial, which suggests that some employers may have kept the efficiency gains and taken back the time. Companies with more white men on staff were also more likely to go back. Industry, country, company size, and remote work made little difference, so the four-day week can work across many kinds of organizations.

The meaning of work

What does work mean to young people in China today—and what has shaped those meanings?

Data
In-depth interviews with young workers in China, 2024; online discussions
Funding
Boston College Research Incentive Grant
Status
In progress

Young people in China are entering work at a time of rapid economic and social change. Talk of “involution” (内卷)—ever harder competition for the same rewards—and of “lying flat” (躺平)—stepping out of that race—has put their relationship to work at the center of public debate. Drawing on in-depth interviews with young workers and on online discussions, I ask what work means to them and how their families, schooling, workplaces, and the times they grew up in have shaped those meanings.

Where

I talked with young workers in person in cities large and small, from Beijing and Shanghai to Yueyang and Hunchun. Each conversation followed a person’s life from childhood and school through every job they had held.

How

Besides talking, each interview used three simple tools on paper.

What I asked

  • Growing up, did your parents have hopes for your future?
  • What do you hope to get from work now? What would your ideal job look like?
  • What would you say to your twenty-year-old self?
